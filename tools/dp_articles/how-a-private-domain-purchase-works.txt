<p>You search for the name you want and it is taken. That is the normal case. Most short, sensible names were registered years ago, and many of them sit unused.</p>
<p>Taken does not mean unavailable. A private purchase is buying a domain directly from the person or company that holds it, usually when it is not advertised for sale. This guide explains how one works in general, whoever runs it. Our own process is described separately in <a href="/how-we-buy-domains/">How we buy domains</a>.</p>

<h2 id="what-taken-means">First, find out what "taken" means</h2>
<p>A registered name is in one of four situations, and each calls for a different approach.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Listed for sale.</strong> The address shows a price or a "make an offer" page. This is a negotiation, but not a private one.</li>
<li><strong>Registered and unused.</strong> No site, or a placeholder. Most private purchases start here.</li>
<li><strong>Registered and in use.</strong> A real business runs on it. This is the hardest case, and often the answer is no.</li>
<li><strong>Expired or about to expire.</strong> Not the shortcut it looks like. ICANN, the body that coordinates domain names, shows an expired name passing through an auto-renew grace period of up to 45 days, a redemption grace period of 30 days, and five days pending deletion before anyone else can register it. The current holder can usually recover it for most of that time.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="who-holds-it">Find out who holds it</h2>
<p>The holder of a domain is called the registrant. ICANN's free lookup tool lets you "look up the current registration data" for a name. It runs on RDAP, the system ICANN has been phasing in since January 2025 to replace the older WHOIS service.</p>
<p>You will often not see a name. Under ICANN's Registration Data Policy, in force since August 2025, registrant details such as name and phone number are commonly redacted. The same policy requires the registrar to publish "an email address or a link to a web form" for reaching the registrant. That form is the legitimate way in.</p>
<p>The lookup still tells you useful things: which registrar holds the name, when it was first registered, and when it expires. The Internet Archive's Wayback Machine, "a service that allows people to visit archived versions of Web sites," shows what the name was used for in the past.</p>

<h2 id="check-the-name">Check the name before you fall for it</h2>
<p><strong>Trademarks.</strong> Owning a domain does not give you the right to use a name someone else has protected. The United States Patent and Trademark Office calls searching its trademark database "one essential step," and the World Intellectual Property Organization runs a Global Brand Database covering many countries. A conflict here is a reason to stop, whatever the price.</p>
<p><strong>History.</strong> Google says "it's fine to use an old domain name for a new, original site that's designed to serve people first." A name's past can still follow it. Google's Search Console help tells people who "recently bought a site that violated our spam policies before you owned it" to fix the problems and then ask for a review. Look at the archive before you buy, not after.</p>
<p><strong>A warning about disputes.</strong> There is a formal procedure for taking a domain from its holder, called the UDRP. It is not a buying tool. WIPO describes it as "only available to resolve disputes between a trademark owner alleging an abusive registration of a domain name and the domain name registrant," and the complainant must prove three things, including that the name was "registered and is being used in bad faith." Wanting a name more than its owner does is not a case.</p>

<h2 id="what-it-is-worth">Decide what it is worth to you</h2>
<p>There is no price list for domains. A sensible range comes from a few questions: what comparable names have sold for, how short and clear the name is, whether the ending suits your market, how the name would be used, and what not having it costs you in confusion and lost visitors.</p>
<p>Then set a maximum and write it down before anyone is contacted. The number should come from the value of the name to your business, not from what the seller asks. If you cannot say what the name is worth to you, you are not ready to negotiate.</p>

<h2 id="first-contact">Make contact without raising the price</h2>
<p>Sellers price to the buyer. An inquiry from a funded company's email address invites a larger number than the same inquiry from an individual. This is why first contact is usually made without naming the buyer, either from a neutral address or through a broker.</p>
<p>Keep the first message short: you are interested in the name, and you would like to know whether the owner would consider selling. No backstory, no budget, no urgency. Expect silence. Many owners answer the second or third polite message, weeks apart.</p>
<p>A broker's role is to do this work for you: research, valuation, outreach, negotiation, and managing the closing. You do not need one for a name with a published price you are happy to pay. One helps most when the name is not for sale and the buyer's identity would move the price.</p>

<h2 id="negotiate">Negotiate against your number, not theirs</h2>
<p>An opening ask is an anchor, not a valuation. The common responses are no reply, "not for sale," and a high counteroffer. Each one is information. Respond to the counter with your reasoning, move in small steps, and stop at the maximum you wrote down.</p>
<p>Where the gap is financing and not price, payment over time is possible. Escrow.com, for example, offers a holding service in which it will "hold the domain(s) in escrow while the Buyer makes scheduled payments."</p>

<p>The buyer gets an inspection period to confirm control of the name, which Escrow.com says "can range between 1 to 30 calendar days." Who pays the escrow fee is part of the deal: the buyer, the seller, or a split. Never send money directly to a stranger for a domain, however reasonable they sound.</p>

<h2 id="the-transfer">The transfer itself</h2>
<p>Two different things are both called a transfer. A <strong>change of registrant</strong> makes you the holder of the name. A <strong>registrar transfer</strong> moves the name from one registrar company to another. ICANN's guidance is that to pass a name to another registrant, "you can initiate a change of registrant by contacting your current registrar."</p>
<p>In practice the quickest route is often for the buyer to open an account at the seller's registrar, take the name there, and move it to a preferred registrar later. The timing rules explain why:</p>
<ul>
<li>Under ICANN's current Transfer Policy, a registrar may refuse a move to another registrar within 60 days of a name's creation or of a previous registrar transfer.</li>
<li>A registrar "must impose a 60-day inter-registrar transfer lock following a Change of Registrant," although it may let the previous holder opt out in advance.</li>
<li>A registrar transfer needs an authorization code, which the registrar must supply within five calendar days of a request.</li>
</ul>
<div class="callout warn" role="note"><p><strong>These rules are changing.</strong> In June 2026 the ICANN Board adopted a revised transfer policy that shortens the waiting periods and removes the lock after a change of registrant. As of November 2026 it had not taken effect and no date had been published. Check the current policy before you plan around a date.</p></div>
<p>When the name arrives, finish the job. Confirm that the registrant is your business, in an account your business controls, at a registrar accredited by ICANN. Turn on the transfer lock and automatic renewal. Keep the contact details accurate, which ICANN requires of every registrant.</p>

<h2 id="common-mistakes">Common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
<li>Revealing who the buyer is, or the budget, in the first message.</li>
<li>Falling for one name with no acceptable alternative, which removes your ability to walk away.</li>
<li>Skipping the trademark and history checks because the name feels right.</li>
<li>Paying outside escrow to save a fee.</li>
<li>Assuming an expired name will be available the next day.</li>
<li>Registering the name to an employee or an agency, not to the business.</li>
<li>Forgetting the renewal date after all that effort.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="walk-away">When to walk away, and what to do instead</h2>
<p>Walk away when the price is above your written maximum, when there is a trademark conflict, when the name's history is a liability, or when the holder is an operating business that depends on it. No one can guarantee that a particular domain can be bought. Some owners never sell.</p>
<p>The alternatives are better than they feel in the moment: a different ending that suits your market, a short qualifying word added to the name, or a new name altogether. You can also keep a standing offer open and revisit it in a year. Circumstances change, and so do owners.</p>
<div class="callout" role="note"><p><strong>The short version.</strong> Research the name and its holder, check trademarks and history, fix your maximum, approach quietly, negotiate against your own number, close through escrow, and make sure the business ends up as the registrant. A name you own outright is worth more than a better one you could not get.</p></div>
