<p>A domain is one of the few identifiers a business can fully control. If software agents are going to act on their own, finding services, paying for them, and delivering results, they will probably need identifiers too. This article asks whether naming systems could do that job.</p>
<div class="callout" role="note"><p><strong>Read this as research.</strong> It describes proposals that are still drafts and two small experiments run by our founder. Lucky Domains does not sell agent identity, and nothing here is a prediction.</p></div>

<h2 id="what-a-domain-does">What a domain already does for a business</h2>
<p>A domain does more than point to a website. Email, profiles, structured data, and press mentions all refer back to it, which is why a search engine or an AI system can use it to decide which company a mention belongs to.</p>
<p>It also carries the web's working machinery for trust. When a certificate authority issues a certificate, it ties a cryptographic key to a domain name, so a browser can check that it is talking to a server that controls that domain. Let's Encrypt describes issuing "a certificate for example.com" once control of the domain has been shown. Email works the same way: the DKIM standard "permits a person, role, or organization that owns the signing domain to claim some responsibility for a message."</p>
<p>In both cases the question being answered is narrower than "who is this, really?" It is "does this come from whoever controls this name?" The real-world identity of that party, and whether it is competent or trustworthy, has to be established some other way: registration records, contracts, reputation, or regulation. Control of a name is the model people are now trying to extend to agents, and it carries the same limit.</p>

<h2 id="what-an-agent-needs">What an identity for an agent would need to carry</h2>
<p>Set aside the technology and the list is familiar. An agent that offers a service, or pays for one, needs most of what a business needs.</p>

<p>None of the entries in the right-hand column is an agreed standard. They fall into two families.</p>

<h2 id="domain-anchored">Family one: anchor the agent to a domain</h2>
<p>The first family reuses what businesses already have.</p>
<p>The Agent2Agent protocol, created by Google and now a Linux Foundation project, defines an Agent Card as "a JSON metadata document published by an A2A Server," and gives it a conventional home: a file at a well-known address on the server's own domain. Whoever controls the domain controls what the card says.</p>
<p>The Model Context Protocol, which Anthropic donated to a Linux Foundation fund in December 2025, takes a similar line in its registry, currently in preview. The registry's documentation lists "Namespace management through DNS verification" among its functions: to publish under a domain's name, you show that you control the domain.</p>
<p>The Internet Engineering Task Force, which maintains core internet standards, has chartered a working group called Discovery of Agents With Names. Its charter says it "will consider DNS and mDNS as mechanisms upon which to build a discovery protocol." Two individual drafts sit alongside it: Agent Name Service v2, subtitled "A Domain-Anchored Identity Layer for Autonomous AI Agents" with a co-author from GoDaddy, and DNS for AI Discovery, with authors from Infoblox, Deutsche Telekom, Amazon, and GoDaddy. Both are individual submissions that expire in spring 2027. Neither has been adopted by the working group.</p>

<h2 id="blockchain-naming">Family two: names that carry payment with them</h2>
<p>The second family starts from blockchain naming, most visibly the Ethereum Name Service, or ENS. An ENS name can hold addresses and what its documentation calls text records: "key-value pairs that can be used to store any arbitrary data associated with a name." A name can therefore point to a wallet and publish a description in the same place.</p>
<p>Two draft specifications build on that. ERC-8004, titled Trustless Agents, proposes "three lightweight registries" for identity, reputation, and validation. ENSIP-25 proposes "a standardized method for directly verifying, using text records, the association between an ENS name and an AI agent identity." Both are drafts.</p>
<p>Payment has its own effort. The x402 protocol, contributed by Coinbase to a foundation launched under the Linux Foundation in July 2026, is "built around the HTTP 402 Payment Required status code," a part of the web's design that went largely unused for decades.</p>
<p>The two families are not rivals in principle. An agent could be discoverable through a domain and paid through a wallet. Whether they converge is one of the open questions.</p>

<h2 id="what-ken-tested">What our founder has tested in public</h2>
<p>Ken Ashe has run two small experiments in the second family and published the full build records on KenAshe.ai. They are his own research. They are not Lucky Domains products, and no client is involved.</p>
<p><strong>ZeroHuman</strong> is, in the words of its build record, "an always-on service that sells two small jobs to other AI agents for 0.0001 ETH each." Its ENS name, zerohuman.eth, "is the storefront, the payment address, and the sender of every result." A text record on the name publishes the menu. The service is listed in the ERC-8004 agent registry as agent #56626 and linked back from the name with an ENSIP-25 record. It also publishes an Agent Card, so it uses pieces from both families.</p>
<p><strong>An earlier experiment</strong>, on November 10, 2026, had two AI agents, each with its own Ethereum wallet, complete one purchase on the Ethereum main network, with a text record on an ENS name acting as the storefront.</p>

<h2 id="what-it-does-not-show">What those tests do not show</h2>
<p>The limits are stated in the build records and matter more than the results.</p>
<ul>
<li>As of November 20, 2026, no outside agent had bought from ZeroHuman. Both paid orders came from Ken's own test buyer.</li>
<li>In the earlier experiment, Ken operated both agents, on one machine.</li>
<li>ZeroHuman has no escrow. A buyer pays first, and refunds are manual.</li>
<li>The registry link rests on two specifications that are still drafts.</li>
<li>The name and its records show that orders and results come from the key behind zerohuman.eth. They do not, by themselves, show who operates the service or whether it deserves trust. A reader learns that from the build record, not from the name.</li>
</ul>
<p>The honest reading is narrow. The pieces can be assembled into something that works end to end. That is not evidence that agents will buy from each other, or that this is the design the market will choose.</p>

<h2 id="open-questions">What is unresolved</h2>
<p><strong>Which kind of name.</strong> Domains are where businesses already are, with registrars, renewal rules, and dispute procedures built over decades. Blockchain names carry payment natively and can be updated by software. It is not yet clear which matters more to an agent, or whether both will be used.</p>
<p><strong>What a name proves.</strong> Proof of control is narrow. Showing control of a domain, or of the cryptographic key behind a name, shows that whoever is acting holds that domain or key. It does not show which person or company that is in the real world, that the operator is competent, or that it can be trusted. Certificates have the same limit for websites: a certificate issued after a domain-control check confirms control of the domain, not the conduct of whoever runs the site.</p>
<p><strong>Who is accountable.</strong> A summary of public comments published in September 2026 by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology reported near unanimity that agents need verifiable identities, and that "there was no consensus on the exact means for doing this." The OpenID Foundation has said autonomous agents require "a fundamental rethinking of identity and authorization."</p>
<p><strong>Whether the drafts survive.</strong> Several of the documents cited here expire or change within months. Any of them could be replaced.</p>
<p><strong>Whether there is a market.</strong> All of this assumes agents will transact with each other at meaningful scale. That has not been shown.</p>

<h2 id="for-a-business-today">What this means for a business today</h2>
<p>There is nothing to buy and no deadline. Two things are worth doing anyway, because they are useful whether or not any of this arrives.</p>
<p>First, hold your domain properly: registered to the business, in an account the business controls. The domain-anchored proposals described above assume that whoever controls the name is the one in charge.</p>
<p>Second, keep your identity consistent and verifiable. The signals that help search engines and AI systems recognize a business now, a clear website, accurate structured data, and profiles that agree, are the same ones any future lookup by software would lean on.</p>
